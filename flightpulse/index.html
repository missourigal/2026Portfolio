01 · The problem

Pilots were flying blind on their own data

Commercial pilots make dozens of high-stakes decisions in the 60–90 minutes before departure — fuel loads, routing, threat awareness, holding estimates. The data they needed most was scattered across disconnected systems, buried in PDFs, or simply unavailable.

Qantas and GE partnered to change that. The goal: a module for the Electronic Flight Bag that gave pilots access to their airline's historical data lake — aggregate flight metrics, safety events, fuel patterns, holding data — surfaced at exactly the right moment in preflight planning.

The challenge wasn't making the data available. It was making it trustworthy, scannable and actionable under real conditions: a dark cockpit, limited time, high cognitive load, and strong scepticism toward yet another app.

My role

I was the UX lead on FlightPulse Preflight, responsible for the full design effort from first research session to final handoff, and managed a UX designer and a UX researcher on the project. I led all of the research, ran both design thinking workshops, and owned the entire design phase — from journey maps and wireframes through to the final look and feel and interaction specs.

The ask

Design an iPad app for the Electronic Flight Bag that gives commercial airline pilots access to their airline's aggregate historical flight data for pre-departure planning — helping them make better fuel decisions and surface safety risks before they become problems.

The constraint

Pilots were already overwhelmed. Too many apps, too many data sources, too little time. FlightPulse Preflight had to earn its place on the EFB or it would be ignored entirely.

02 · Research

Understanding fifty steps inside a cockpit we'd never flown

I led all generative and evaluative research for the project, working with Qantas pilot SMEs and interviewing pilots across multiple airlines. The findings forced us to rethink our initial assumptions.

The journey map

I mapped the full pilot-in-command journey — from signing on for duty through post-flight shutdown — across 50 tasks, 7 flight stages, and environmental factors including noise, workspace constraints and light conditions.

This wasn't a typical journey map. The goal was to identify exactly where in the preflight sequence data could change a decision — and where it would be irrelevant, ignored, or actively harmful to surface.

Two pilots, two relationships to data

John Long haul

Flies Sydney/LA and Sydney/Guangzhou. Starts planning two to three days out. Highly data-motivated — wants to make the right fuel decisions to reduce operational cost. The fleet is his family.

Henry Short haul

Flies Sydney–Melbourne twenty times a week. Extremely route-familiar — rarely uses airline apps, relying on fellow pilots and habit. An overabundance of data apps has made him resistant to new tools.

Four insights that shaped everything

High noise-to-signal ratio

Pilots make scores of complicated decisions in a short window with too many data sources. More information wasn't the answer — better-filtered information was.

Safety and fuel are inseparable

Taking on more fuel feels like a safety decision, not an efficiency one. Habitually loading extra fuel reflects the anxiety of the unknown — which data could address.

Pilots trust people over systems

In uncertainty, pilots ask a trusted colleague, not a computer. The design needed to feel like it surfaced collective pilot wisdom, not cold algorithmic output.

Context matters as much as data

"I don't just want to know an event occurred — I want to know why, so I can do something about it." Data without explanation was noise.

03 · Design process

From 25 ideas to 5 features in nine months

Design ran in parallel with continuous research, never sequentially — two three-day design thinking workshops with the full 19-person cross-company team, pilot validation at an EFB industry conference, and 90-minute think-aloud sessions with pilots across multiple airlines.

Every decision was pressure-tested against the real constraints of the cockpit: a pilot thirty minutes from departure, wearing a headset, in a small space, under ambient light, who needs to get in and out of the app in under three minutes.

The three-day workshop

I planned and facilitated both workshops, bringing engineers, data scientists, safety analysts and pilots from GE and Qantas into the same room for three days.

Day one

Understanding the goals

The full team reviewed research findings, business requirements and pilot requirements. Pilot SMEs guided everyone through what actually matters in the cockpit.

Day two

Defining the MVP

Prioritised 25+ product ideas on an importance/effort matrix, balancing what pilots wanted against what engineering could ship by March 2020.

Day three

Sketching

Teams sketched MVP ideas for Threats & Events, Holding Fuel and the Fuel Wildcard — shared, critiqued, re-sketched, and left with concepts to validate.

Four principles, drawn from pilots rather than convention

  1. 01Save me timeWith massive information volume and limited time, any shortcut is a feature. Showing fuel burn filtered by fleet saved pilots the mental load of converting time into fuel.
  2. 02Recent above seasonalWhat happened in the past two to four weeks matters more to a decision than year-over-year trends. Recency became a first-class filter throughout the UI.
  3. 03Transparency of limitationsFor historical aggregates, being explicit about how a number was calculated — and what its limits are — let pilots make their own judgement call. Trust was earned, not assumed.
  4. 04Less context is moreShow the average. Let the pilot decide whether today is unusual. "Here is the information, here is how we got it — do with it what you want." No over-interpretation.

04 · Validation

The MVP that wasn't good enough, and the pivot that made it ship

Our first round of pilot validation was humbling. The initial MVP didn't have enough content to earn a place on the EFB, and pilots said so directly. Rather than argue, I went back to the drawing board.

Round one — initial concept testing

Tested low-fidelity sketches and early wireframes with pilots from three airlines. The feedback was consistent: the MVP felt thin. Pilots started telling us what they actually needed, which opened the door to significant scope expansion.

I documented every additional need, knowing we couldn't build them all. The goal was to understand the full picture before deciding what to cut.

Round two — EFB conference + 10 pilot sessions

I turned the expanded concepts into a clickable prototype and took it to an EFB industry conference for desirability feedback, then ran 90-minute think-aloud sessions with 10 Qantas pilots, both long-haul and short-haul.

The ask: prioritise every feature down to a top five. The result — their top five included all four of our MVP ideas. That gave me the case to argue we could do all five in time.

What we learned, feature by feature

Threats & events

Most valuable to Qantas

Pilots wanted a text list explaining events and why they occur — not icons or graphics, which go stale as events are added and removed. The context behind an event mattered as much as the event.

Holding data

Duration and fuel, not location

Pilots don't need to know where holding patterns are. They need to know how long holding typically runs for their arrival time and how much fuel it burns. A "Google-style" time-of-day chart — sketched by a Qantas pilot — became the model.

Planned vs actual fuel

Drill-down by phase of flight

High-level averages were interesting; the breakdown was useful. Taxi out, climb, en route — understanding where the variance happened beat knowing that variance existed.

Map / vertical profile

Dropped from the MVP — correctly

Neither group found maps necessary. "You fly the route you are dispatched to fly." Historical flight paths were mildly interesting but not decision-relevant. Cutting it simplified the app without losing pilot value.

06 · What I'd take forward

Three things this project taught me

  1. 01Pilot scepticism was a design constraint, not a problem to solveThe resistance to "yet another app" forced the product to be genuinely useful rather than merely functional. Designing for adoption meant designing for trust, which meant radical transparency about how data was derived and where it stopped being reliable.
  2. 02Scope creep from user research is a signal, not a failureWhen the initial MVP generated "scope creep," it was because pilots were telling us the original scope wasn't enough. The key was documenting everything, then using a second validation round to let pilots prioritise — turning their demands into our roadmap argument.
  3. 03Cross-company design teams need structural trust-buildingWorking across GE and Qantas meant two cultures, two technical stacks and two sets of priorities. The design thinking workshops were the single best tool for getting engineers, data scientists, safety analysts and pilots speaking the same language about what the product needed to do.
